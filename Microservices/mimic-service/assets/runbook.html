Runbook · Order Platform

One entry per verdict the live panel can reach. It links straight here, to the entry matching what it decided — so the question is never ''which page is this?'' but ''what do I do about this?''. Every entry says what the panel saw, what to do in order, and why it is worth doing.

Cannot tell

The panel says: Prometheus is not answering.

  1. Treat every number on the panel as the last one that arrived, not the current one. Nothing below the banner can be trusted until this clears.
  2. Check the container is up: docker compose ps prometheus
  3. Read why it stopped: docker compose logs --tail=50 prometheus
  4. Restart it: docker compose restart prometheus
  5. Confirm every target is being scraped again at the targets page.

This outranks every other verdict on purpose. A monitoring system that has stopped receiving measurements will happily keep showing the last healthy picture, and ''everything looks fine'' is the most dangerous thing a panel can say when it has stopped being told anything. The lamps go grey rather than green for the same reason.

Down

The panel says: a component is not answering.

  1. Find the red lamp on the drawing. That names the component.
  2. Confirm it: DevStatus.cmd
  3. Read the last of its output: docker compose logs --tail=100 <service>
  4. If it is restarting in a loop, the logs repeat — look at the first failure after each start, not the last line.
  5. Restart it: docker compose restart <service>
  6. If it will not start at all, the whole stack can be rebuilt with DevStart.cmd. That is a bigger hammer than it looks: it leaves data alone, but every service restarts.

A service that is down loses nothing permanently here. The outbox holds accepted orders in PostgreSQL and the broker holds unprocessed commands, so work waits rather than disappearing. What it does mean is that the queue behind it is growing — check waiting for the worker on the panel before deciding how urgent this is.

Falling behind

The panel says: one stage is not keeping up with the one before it.

  1. Read which stage the banner names. It names the first one that fell behind, which is the one to look at — everything downstream of a stalled stage is starved rather than broken.
  2. Look at that stage's own readings on the drawing: throughput, and for the worker also slowest one in a hundred.
  3. Check whether its queue is growing. A stage that is behind with a growing queue is saturated; behind with an empty queue is stuck.
  4. Read its logs: docker compose logs --tail=100 <service>
  5. If it is saturated rather than broken, add capacity: docker compose up -d --scale worker-service=4

This is the most useful thing the panel can tell you, and it comes from a conservation law rather than a threshold: every order accepted must be relayed, every relayed order processed, and every processed order reacted to. In steady state those five rates are the same number. Where they stop matching is where the work is going missing, and that localises the fault to one component without anybody comparing five numbers by eye.

Degraded

The panel says: the chain balances, but a component reports trouble.

  1. Find the amber lamp. Its detail line says what tripped it — usually the ninety-ninth percentile processing time, or a queue past its threshold.
  2. Check the direction: a reading marked ▲ is getting worse, and one marked ▼ is recovering on its own.
  3. If it is worsening, treat it as ''falling behind'' above before it becomes that.
  4. If it is recovering, watch it. A brief spike after a restart or a burst of traffic is normal and needs nobody.

Degraded means slow, not broken: work is still flowing end to end. That is why it ranks below a stage falling behind — this is a warning about where things are heading, and that is a statement about where they already are.

Work is parked

The panel says: messages failed three times and were set aside.

  1. Look first, change nothing: DevReplay.cmd --dry-run
  2. Decide whether the cause is fixed. Replaying into a system that still rejects them just parks them again three attempts later.
  3. When it is fixed, put them back: DevReplay.cmd
  4. Watch parked as dead letters fall, and the worker's throughput rise as it works through the backlog.

Nothing clears these on its own, and that is deliberate rather than an oversight. A message that failed three times is a question — bad data, a schema that moved, a downstream service that was away — and retrying it forever would turn one broken message into a loop that buries everything behind it. The count stays on screen until a person decides.

Normal

The panel says: everything is answering and the chain balances.

  1. Nothing to do.

Worth stating explicitly, because a panel that only ever speaks up when something is wrong leaves you unable to tell ''fine'' from ''not looking''. If the readings are all zero the system is idle rather than broken — drive some traffic from the console's left-hand rail to see it move.
